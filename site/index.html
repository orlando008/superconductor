An open research platform

Why does copper oxide superconduct?

Forty years after they were discovered, nobody can fully explain why certain copper-based ceramics conduct electricity perfectly, losing no energy at all, at temperatures far warmer than any other material manages at normal pressure. We're building an open-source framework that lets anybody, together with their AI agents, help make progress on this question. You don't need to be a scientist: the work is split into small, clearly defined tasks, and every contribution is checked automatically.

An independent, community-built project working on Grand Challenge Problem 01: A Predictive Theory of Cuprate Superconductivity, posed by Discovered Materials.

The puzzle

Superconductors need extreme cold. Cuprates need much less.

A superconductor is a material that carries electricity with no resistance at all, so no energy is lost as heat. Superconductors already run MRI scanners, particle accelerators and maglev trains. The catch is that each one only works below a certain temperature. Classic superconductors need to be within a few degrees of absolute zero (−273 °C, the coldest anything can get), which takes expensive liquid helium.

In 1986, a family of copper-based ceramics called cuprates broke that barrier. Within a few years some worked above −196 °C, the temperature of liquid nitrogen, a coolant that costs less than milk. The accepted theory of superconductivity, from 1957, doesn't explain them. Without the right theory, nobody can design better materials on purpose, including one that might work at room temperature.

Why it's hard

Electrons that behave like a crowd are very hard to calculate

In most materials each electron can be treated as if it moves on its own. In cuprates the electrons repel each other so strongly that they move as a collective, the way a packed crowd does. You cannot follow one person and understand the crowd.

Physicists simplify the material to its most important layer: a flat grid of copper atoms. Even then, each spot on the grid (a "site") can be empty, hold one electron of either kind (the two kinds are called spin up and spin down), or hold two. With four possibilities per site, every site you add multiplies the number of possible arrangements by four.

The simplified picture: three dials

The standard simplified picture is called the Hubbard model, and it's what the grid at the top of this page shows. Electrons sit on a checkerboard of copper sites and do two things: they hop to neighbouring sites, and they push each other away when two land on the same site. Three dials describe it: how easily electrons hop, how strongly they repel each other, and how many electrons have been removed, which scientists call doping.

With exactly one electron per site and strong repulsion, the electrons jam. None can move without crowding onto an occupied site, so the material can't conduct electricity at all: it's an insulator. Remove a few electrons and they can move again.

That's where real cuprates get interesting. Removing electrons doesn't just unjam them: in the right range, the material becomes a superconductor. Remove too few and nothing special happens. Remove a bit more and it starts to superconduct, and the more you remove, the warmer the temperature at which it keeps working, up to a point. Remove too many and the effect fades away again. Draw the warmest working temperature against the share of electrons removed and you get a hill. Scientists call it the "superconducting dome":

The big open question: does this simple picture produce superconductivity by itself, or is something missing? Large computer studies in recent years disagree, depending on the method used and on fine details such as how easily electrons hop diagonally. Settling questions like this is what this project is for.

Checking

No method works everywhere, so we make them check each other's work

Think about measuring distances. A tape measure is exact, but only across a room. A laser rangefinder reaches across a field, but can be fooled by bright sunlight. GPS works anywhere, but is only accurate to a few metres. None of them is right everywhere. Where their ranges overlap, though, they should give the same reading. If they don't, one of them is broken.

Calculating how electrons behave works the same way. Exact answers are impossible for anything but tiny grids, so scientists use a range of shortcut methods. Each one is trustworthy in some situations and unreliable in others. A single method's answer can't tell you whether you are seeing real physics or the method failing.

The main techniques for calculating how electrons behave

The perfect calculator

Exact diagonalization
Works well
Checks every possible arrangement of electrons, so its answers are exact.
Breaks down
Can only handle tiny grids of about 20 sites. Beyond that there are too many arrangements to check.

The random sampler

Quantum Monte Carlo
Works well
Like an opinion poll: samples huge numbers of arrangements at random instead of checking them all, so it handles bigger grids.
Breaks down
Once electrons are removed, which is exactly where superconductivity happens, the samples become so noisy that it would need impossibly many.

The strip specialist

DMRG and tensor networks
Works well
Very accurate on long, thin strips of the grid.
Breaks down
The real material is a wide, flat sheet. Getting from strips to a full sheet involves some guesswork.

The zoom lens

Dynamical mean-field theory
Works well
Studies one site or a small patch in great detail, treating everything around it as a blurred average.
Breaks down
Misses patterns that stretch across many sites.

The AI approach

Neural-network quantum states
Works well
A neural network learns to describe the electrons. Its accuracy is improving fast.
Breaks down
It's hard to tell whether the network found the best answer or got stuck on a worse one.

The step-by-step builder

Diagrammatic Monte Carlo
Works well
Builds the answer up in pieces from a simple starting point, and can describe an infinitely large grid.
Breaks down
When electrons repel strongly, the pieces stop adding up to a reliable answer.

The plan

Seven layers, each built on the ones below

Hunting directly for the answer leaves little behind if the hunt fails. Building reliable, checkable tools is useful whatever happens: research groups can use them today, future AI systems start from firmer ground, and the same tools work for other materials where electrons behave like a crowd.

It also matches how the Grand Challenge judges explanations: on how well they predict experimental results they weren't tuned to match, using code or working that others can rerun.

Results

A result counts once someone else reproduces it

Anyone can submit a calculation. Every record is permanent and stamped with a unique code that changes if anyone alters it, so it can't be quietly edited later. Each record names the person responsible for it. Whether a result counts as trusted is worked out from what other people find, never declared by the person who submitted it.

  1. Submitted

    A contributor runs a calculation and records it with everything needed to rerun it: the exact problem, the code version and the settings.

  2. Reproduced

    A different person reruns it independently. Using a different method counts as even stronger evidence.

  3. Trusted

    The result becomes something others can build on, compare theories against and cite.

If a reproduction disagrees, the result is marked disputed and investigated in the open. Disagreements are useful: each one is either a mistake to fix or something new to understand. Nobody settles one by editing the numbers.
